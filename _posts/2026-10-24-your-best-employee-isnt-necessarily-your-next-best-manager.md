When a company is growing quickly, promoting from within often feels like the obvious choice.

You have someone who consistently delivers. They know the company. They understand the product. They solve problems before anyone asks. They’ve earned the trust of the team.

So when a management position opens up, the decision seems easy:

**Promote your best performer.**

But being exceptional at the work and being able to lead the people doing the work are two very different skill sets.

And when we confuse the two, we can unintentionally set up some of our strongest employees—and their teams—to struggle.

## The Skills That Got Them Here Aren’t the Skills They Need Next

High performers are often rewarded for being independent, decisive, efficient, and technically strong.

Leadership asks something different of them.

Suddenly, success isn’t only about what they accomplish. It’s about their ability to help other people accomplish.

They have to learn how to give feedback instead of simply fixing the problem themselves.

They have to ask questions when they already think they know the answer.

They have to navigate conflict, understand different personalities, delegate work they could probably do faster themselves, and recognize when their own frustration or assumptions are influencing how they respond.

That transition can be significant.

Yet in many organizations, we change someone’s title without giving them the space or support to change how they lead.

## A Promotion Isn’t Leadership Development

Imagine your strongest engineer becomes an engineering manager.

Yesterday, when something went wrong, their job was to solve it.

Today, their job may be to help someone else think through the problem, develop confidence, and eventually solve it without them.

That requires restraint.

It requires curiosity.

It requires reflection.

And those skills rarely appear automatically with a new title.

Without intentional development, a new manager may fall back on what has always made them successful: taking over, giving answers, working harder, or expecting everyone else to approach problems the way they do.

Not because they don’t care about their team.

Because that is what they know.

## Before You Promote, Look Beyond Performance

Instead of asking only, **“Who is our strongest performer?”**, organizations should also be asking:

Who shows curiosity about other people’s perspectives?

Who can give feedback without making it personal?

Who creates space for others rather than needing to have the answer?

Who can examine their own impact when something isn’t working?

Who genuinely wants to develop people—not simply advance their own career?

Technical expertise matters. Credibility matters. Performance matters.

But leadership requires another layer: the willingness and ability to understand yourself while learning how to bring out the best in someone else.

## And Then, Develop Them

Choosing the right person is only half of the equation.

New managers need opportunities to reflect on how they are showing up, practice difficult conversations, receive feedback, recognize their patterns, and develop their own leadership identity.

Leadership development shouldn’t begin six months after someone starts struggling.

It should be part of the transition into leadership itself.

Because when we promote a high performer without developing the leader inside them, we risk losing two things at once:

**A great individual contributor—and the potential for a great manager.**

The goal isn’t to stop promoting your best people.

It’s to stop assuming that being great at one role automatically prepares someone for the next.

## 3 Questions to Reflect On

1. **When we promote someone into leadership, what are we actually evaluating—performance in their current role or readiness to lead people?**
1. **What support do our new managers receive to help them shift from doing the work themselves to developing others?**
1. **Are we creating a culture where managers are expected to already know how to lead—or one where leadership is something they are continually encouraged to reflect on, practice, and refine?**

**Great performers create results. Great leaders create the conditions for other people to create them too.**
