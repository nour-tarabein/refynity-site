Most companies don’t ignore leadership development.

They invest in it.

They send managers to workshops. They bring in speakers. They give leaders frameworks for communication, feedback, delegation, and difficult conversations.

And for a few days afterward, everyone feels energized.

Then Monday happens.

The difficult employee walks into the manager’s office. A deadline gets missed. Someone becomes defensive during a conversation. The team is understaffed. The manager is overwhelmed

And suddenly, the framework they learned last week feels very far away.

The problem isn’t necessarily the training.

The problem is what happens after the training.

## Knowing Something Isn’t the Same as Being Able to Practice It

Leadership development is often designed around knowledge transfer.

Here’s how to give feedback.

Here’s how to coach.

Here’s how to delegate.

Here’s how to navigate conflict.

Those skills matter. But knowing what good leadership looks like and being able to access those skills in a real, emotionally charged moment are two very different things.

A manager may understand that they should approach a difficult conversation with curiosity.

But when an employee becomes defensive, that manager may become defensive too.

They may avoid the conversation altogether.

They may soften the feedback so much that the message disappears.

Or they may become overly directive because getting the task done feels easier than slowing down and coaching someone through it.

This is where traditional leadership training often falls short.

It teaches managers what to do without creating enough space for them to understand how they actually show up while doing it.

And that distinction matters.

## Leadership Development Requires Reflection

Managers don’t lead in a vacuum.

They bring their own communication patterns, assumptions, stress responses, strengths, blind spots, and experiences into every interaction.

That means sustainable leadership development requires more than learning a new technique.

It requires reflection.

After a difficult conversation, for example, a reflective manager doesn’t simply ask:

Did I handle that correctly?

They might ask:

What was happening for me during that conversation?

At what point did I stop being curious?

What assumptions was I making about the other person?

What impact did my approach have?

What would I want to do differently next time?

Those questions turn everyday leadership experiences into opportunities for development.

Instead of waiting for the next workshop, managers begin learning from the work they are already doing.

## Reflection Is What Makes Refinement Possible

Reflection alone isn’t the goal.

The goal is what reflection allows a leader to do next.

Refine.

Maybe a manager realizes they consistently avoid giving direct feedback because they want to be liked.

Maybe they recognize that when they feel pressure, they stop delegating and start taking everything back onto their own plate.

Maybe they discover that they enter conversations believing they already know why an employee behaved a certain way.

Those realizations create choice.

And choice creates the opportunity to practice a different response.

This is how leadership behaviors actually begin to change: not through one moment of insight, but through a continuous cycle of noticing, reflecting, adjusting, practicing, and noticing again.

Reflect. Refine. Lead.

Over time, the learning becomes less about remembering a framework and more about developing a different way of leading.

## The Missing Infrastructure Around Managers

This is also why companies have to stop treating leadership development as an event.

A two-hour workshop can introduce an idea.

It cannot create a leadership culture by itself.

Managers need opportunities to revisit what they are learning, talk through real situations, receive feedback, practice new behaviors, and reflect on what is and isn’t working.

That can happen through reflective supervision, intentional 1:1s, leadership cohorts, coaching, peer reflection, or simple rituals built into the organization.

The format can vary.

What matters is the consistency.

Because the real test of leadership development isn’t whether managers enjoyed the training.

It isn’t whether they completed the course.

And it isn’t whether they can repeat the framework.

The real test is whether something changes when they walk back into the room with their team.

Do they ask a different question?

Do they stay present a little longer during discomfort?

Do they give the feedback they used to avoid?

Do they pause before reacting?

Do their employees experience them differently?

That is where leadership development becomes leadership practice.

And ultimately, that is what creates stronger managers, stronger teams, and organizations capable of growing without leaving their people behind.

## Reflection Questions for Your Organization

1. What happens after your managers receive leadership training? Where are they expected to revisit, practice, or reflect on what they learned?

2. What leadership behaviors keep repeating despite the training you’ve already provided? Consider what those patterns may be telling you about the support your managers actually need.

3. Where have you created consistent space for managers to reflect on how they are leading — not just what they are accomplishing? If that space doesn’t currently exist, what would it look like to build it into the way your organization develops leaders?
